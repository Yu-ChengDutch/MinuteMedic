Introduction

This section will focus on alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, cocaine, synthetic stimulants ((meth)amphetamines and synthetic cathinones), MDMA, heroin and opioids, new psychoactive substances (i.e., semi-synthetic cannabinoids, synthetic cathinones, synthetic opioids, and nitazenes), and a miscellaneous category (LSD, hallucinogenic mushrooms, ketamine, GHB, and nitrous oxide).

Trade and traffic

The drug trade increasingly uses the international trade network, infiltrating logistical changes using e.g., container switching, semi-submersibles, chemical conceilment of shipments etc. They then often process the raw materials (which often come from south America or China) within Europe, especially the Netherlands and Belgium, and Poland (for synthetic cathinones). Cannabis cultivation occurs on large scale within Europe, as does the synthetic production of amphetamine, methamphetamine, synthetic cathinones, MDMA, cocaine and heroin. The previous yeears have seen a sharp increase in the use of cocaine, while drugs like MDMA and methamphetamines have declined. Cannabis, however, remains the most consumed illicit drug.

Legal drugs

Alcohol

Alcohol is a CNS depressant which works by facilitating GABA's actions in the GABA-a receptor. Both dopamine and endogenous opioids play a role in alcohol addiction. It is metabolised by the liver, a capacity which is already saturated after one standard drink, into a metabolite (acetaldehyde) which is carcinogenic. This is then broken down into acetate, which is broken down into CO2 and water, which is eliminated through urine and breath. 5-10% of ethanol is excreted unchanged in breath, urine, and sweat. Dependance leads to many negative effects, including impaired memory and emotional processing (incl. prosody perception and theory of mind) leading to impaired social function. It might also lead to MDD and psychosis (the latter through a kindling mechanism).

Withdrawal may be associated with anxiety, shakiness, sweating, vomiting, fast heart rate, and a mild fever (all of these 6 to 12 hours after last drink). In more severe cases, seizures (peak at 24-36 hours) and delirium tremens (peak at 48-72 hours) may occur. Benzodiazepines may commonly be employed to calm the now overactive nervous system, with thiamiine routinely being added. Patient should be monitored for electrolyte problems and hypoglycaemia. Withdrawal hallucinosis may occur! Severe symptoms set on 12-24 hours after last drink. Sometimes, withdrawal symptoms can linger up to a year which is then referred to as "protracted withdrawal" or "post-acute withdrawal syndrome". These may include a craving for alcohol, anhedonia, clouding of sensorium, disorientation, nausea and vomiting, or headache. Insomnia is common as well, for which magnesium or Trazodone may help.

Tobacco

Nicotine is a stimulant and anxiolytic, belonging to the alkaloids. It binds to the nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, and thus releases dopamine, acetylcholine, and norepinephrine, producing alertness, reduced anxiety, and mild euphoria. Dependance is associated with many negative health effects. Overdose is rare, but throug hexcess stimulation it results in a biphasic pattern of initial symptoms (excessive salivation, abdominal pain, pallor, sweating, fasciculations, even seizures) and a later period of depressor efects (hypotension, bradycardia, CNS depression, and even coma with difficulty breathing).

Withdrawal may look like intense cravings, anger or irritability, anxiety, depression, impatience, insomnia, restlessness, hunger, weight gain, and trouble focussing. Even flu-like symptoms and mouth ulcers may occur! The most common way to stop is nicotine replacement therapy with a taper.

Cannabis

Cannabis is a psychoactive substance that interacts with the endocannabinoid system. The primary psychoactive compound in cannabis is delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), which binds to cannabinoid receptors in the brain and body. Cannabis use can lead to various effects, including altered perception, mood changes, and cognitive impairment. Long-term use may result in dependence (roughly 9% of users; significantly less than alcohol)and withdrawal symptoms (irritability, anxiety, insomnia, and depression) when discontinued.

Illicit drugs

Cocaine

Cocaine is a stimulant and tropane alkaloid, and often consumed snorted, injected, or smoked as crack cocaine. It inhibits reuptake of serotonin-norepinephrine-dopamine, and thus induces euphoria, alertness, concentration, libido, and reduces fatigue and apetite. Acute use may result in vasoconstriction, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, or seizures. Overdose may lead to stroke, heart attack, or sudden cardiac death. Negative mental effects include agitation, paranoia, anxiety, irritability, psychosis, hallucinations, delusions, violence, and suicidal/homicidal thinking. Many chornic users develop a "transient manic-like condition" similar to schizophrenia, often with "coke bugs"-sensation (i.e., the hallucination that there are insects under their skin). Physically, it often comes with nasal septum perforation ("coke nose") when snorted.

Withdrawal is associated with three different phases:

  1. "Crash": immediatley after cocaine is not being used anymore. Exhaustion, hypersomnia, no cravings to use, dysthymia, increased apetitie, restlessness, and irritability
  2. "Withdrawal": 1-10 weeks after last use. Cravings, anhedonia, dysphoria, anxiety, irritability, and sleep disturbances
  3. "Extinction": 10 weeks to 6 months after last use. Episodic cravings, some dysphoria

Synthetic stimulants

Both metamphetamines and synthetic cathinones (e.g., bath salts, khat (natural), 2/3/4-mmc etc.) work as either monoamine (serotonin-norepinephrine-dopamine) reuptake inhibitors or even transporter reversers. methamphetamine in particular is also an inhibitor of monoamine oxidase, and somehow acts on CNS calcium prompoting a dopamine release. They are used recreationally for their euphoriant, stimulant, and aphrodisiac qualities. It is often used for "chemsex", together with poppers (strong vasodilators that make you dizzy, and dilate e.g., throat and anus). The crash afterwards is marked by hypersomnia and anhedonia.

Psychological withdrawal symptoms are common, may occur within 24 hours after last dose and last up to 4 weeks. They may include craving, dysphoria, fatigue, increased appetite, increased movement or decreased movement, lack of motivation, in- or hypersomnia, and vivid or lucid dreams.

Related is MDMA, which is known as ecstacy in tablet form and molly in crystal form, as it also increases release of serotonin-norepinephrine-dopamine. It brings energy, and may last up to six hours. Acutely, it may result in bruxism, blurred vision, sweating, and tachycardia. Extended use may lead to amnesia, memory disorders, paranoia, and insomnia. It is even less dependence-inducing than cannabis. Typically, it is used infrequently. It is, however, teratogenic.

Heroin and opioids

A synthetic morphin-like opioid made from the latex of the poppy, heroin is often injected because of the facilitated ease of passing the blood-brain barrier (as there is no first-pass effect that changes its efficient structure). There, it is deacetylated into morphin, which binds to the mu-opioid receptors. This leads to euphoria, analgesia, anxiolotica etc. However, due to the large histamine release created by heroin specifically, it leads to a stronger "body high" but also pruritus.

Adverse effects may include nausea, vomiting, pruritus. After the initial effects, users will be drowsy for hours, with clouded mental function, bradycardia, and bradypneu. The latter may lead to coma and permanent brain damage. These substances are extremely psychologically and physically dependance-generating, while also damaging the body's cardiovascular, neurological, and hormonal system. Injection carries with it another set of risks related to blood-borne pathogens, bacterial infections, abscesses, poisoning from contaminants, or nephropathy. Smoking it ("Chasing the dragon"), is obviously not good for the lungs.

Opioid withdrawal begins 8-24 hours after last dose, persisting for 4-10 days. Initial symptoms include agitation, anxiety, muscle ache, increased tears, insomnia, runny nose, excessive sweating and yawning. Late symptoms include abdominal cramps, diarrhea, pupil dilation, goosebumps, nausea, and vomiting.

Overdose is treated with Naloxone or Narcan (a non-selective and competitive opioid receptor antagonis).

Nitazines are opioid-like drugs that are even stronger than fentanyl.

Ketamine

A mixture of the efficient esketamine and inefficient arketamine, it blocks the NMDA-receptor of Glutamate. This blocks central sensitisation of the dorsal horn neurons, and thus interferes with pain transmission in the spinal cord. However, it is used recreationally for its hallucinogenic and dissociative effects, where it is sold in crystalline, powder, and liquid form referred to as "K". High doses lead to pain-relief, sedation, amnesia, and a trance-like state. While liver and urinary toxicity are reported, the long-term effects remain largely unknown. There is moderate to high risk of addition.
