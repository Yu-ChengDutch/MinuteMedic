Bipolar disorder

This is a mood disorder which is characterised by periods of depression and abnormally elevated mood. When this elevation is severe or associated with psychosis, it is referred to as a manic episode. Otherwise, it is called hypomania. Feelings of energy, happiness, or irritability are common, as are sleep disturbances. People with BD are roughly 12 times more likely to die of suicide, as 34% attempt suicide during their lifetime; with 78% of adolescents with BD engaging in self-harm.

The major subtypes are Bipolar I Disorder and Bipolar II Disorder, with bipolar I being characterised by manic episodes with or without depressive eposides, and Bipolar II being characterised by hypomanic eposides with necessarily one major depressive episode.

Age of onset is highly bimodal: 24 and 46 years of age. Only 40-60% are employed, as only 16% are high functioning.

Mania

A bit more on mania: proper mania persists for more than a week, while hypomania lasts at least four consequetive days without always being associated with impaired functioning. The core symptom here, is psychomotor agitation (an increase in energy of psychomotor activity) but often comes with grandiosity, racing thoughts, pressured speech that is hard to interrupt, decreased need for sleep, disinhibited social behaviour, increased goal-oriented activities, and impaired judgement (leading to high-risk behaviours such as excessive spending). It is usually seen as a medical emergency, altough not resulting in violent behaviour by itself. Hypomania is not a psychiatric emergency, as it does not limit functional ability and lacks psychotic features; they rarely progress to full-blown manic episodes. Commonly, these patients show increased creativity or decreased judgement. Often, the patients experiencing this condition are not aware of their change in mood, nor of the effect on their surroundings.

While depressive states are clear, they often last way longer than the manic episodes. For this reason, many people are wrongfully misdiagnosed with having "just" depression.

A mixed-state is especially dangerous, as here depressive feelings of worthlessness and hopelessness are paired with the mood swings, grandiose thoughts, and difficulties with impulse control of mania.

Seasonality occurs in 15% of individuals. Roughly 30% of patients can have their mania induced using sleep deprivation. Childhood trauma is common, and a predictor of negative or violent outcomes.

Treatment

Classically, the pharmacological approach is mood stabilisers (lithium), second generation antipsychotics (e.g., quetiapine), or anticonvulsants (e.g., valproic acid). Various new medication is being trialled, amongst which are anti-inflammatories (e.g., celecoxib) which seem effective even in acute mania, and probiotics which reduce time-in-hospital and lengthen time to reuptake.

Cyclothymia

While Bipolar I has "high highs, and possibly low lows", and Bipolar II has "low highs, and low lows", cyclothymia cycles right between them, never reaching beyond hypomania or subclinical depression.

Dysthymia and hyperthymia

While the aforementioned conditions speak of "episodes", both dysthymia and hyperthymia are much more "personality-like" in the sense that they have to persist more than 2 years in adults. The essential symptom involves the individual feeling depressed for the majority of days, and parts of the day, for at least two years. Low energy, disturbances in sleep or in appetite, and low self-esteem typically contribute to the clinical picture as well. "Double depression", then is an episode of MDD on top of a dysthymic personality, because of which it often goes unrecognised (as the patient accepts a large amount of depressive symptoms as just part of their personality). In general, it is diagnosed very late.
