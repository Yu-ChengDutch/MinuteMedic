Psychosis (BMJ; P. Byrne; 2007)

Psychosis is a "disorder of thinking and perception, where typically people do not ascribe their symptoms to a mental disorder":

  1. Positive symptoms:
    1. Hallucinations: auditory, visual, tactile, olfactory, gustatory
    2. Delusions: persecutory, grandiose, religious, somatic, nihilistic, erotomanic
    3. Disorganized thinking and speech: tangentiality, circumstantiality, derailment, incoherence
  2. Negative symptoms:
    1. Affective flattening: reduced emotional expression
    2. Alogia: poverty of speech
    3. Avolition: lack of motivation and spontaneity (e.g., conversational flow)
    4. Anhedonia: inability to experience pleasure
    5. Asociality: lack of interest in social interactions
    6. Other: lack of personal grooming, poor rapport, apathy (blunted affect)

When it becomes chronic, it may fall within a diagnosis of schizophrenia. However, there is a wide range of other psychiatric differential diagnoses:

  1. Schizophrenia spectrum disorders: acute and transient psychotic disorder, schizophrenia, non-affective psychotic disorders
  2. Mood disorders: bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder with psychotic features
  3. Substance-induced psychotic disorders: alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, hallucinogens, sedatives, and other substances
  4. Personality disorders: schizotypal or paranoid personality disorders
  5. Other: ADHD, Asperger's, OCD, PTSD, delusional disorder, schizoaffective disorder

In general, psychosis is a very tiring and possibly scary experience for patients as they spend all their energy trying to figure out what sensory input is real (and can be trusted) and what sensory input isn't.

Pharmacological sedation should be done with benzodiazepines, and not with antipsychotics (which should only be used for their proper effect). Herein, follow the protocols of your institution. Typically, antipsychotics come with extrapyramidal side effects, and raised prolactin (sexual dysfunction and galactorrhoea), anticholinergic (dry mouth, tachycardia, urinary obstruction), and antiadrenergic (postural hypotension, impotence) side effects. The atypical ones only start displaying these at supra-therapeutic doses. All of them cause some level of sedation, and lower seizure threshold (especially CLOZAPINE). Except for ZIPRASIDONE and ARIPIPRAZOLE, all antipsychotics cause weight gain and impaired glucose tolerance. All of them raise the risk of thromboembolic disease. Investigation into the side effects should re-occur, as they may occur later.

Continued treatment is only effective if medication is combined with family education and social skills training. Their home environment being stressful has a significant negative impact, and should thus be avoided.

Schizophrenia spectrum

Properly, the spectrum encompasses all diseases in which psychosis is a main feature. This encompases schizophrenia, and schizophreniform disorders (i.e., lasting less than 6 months and possibly accompanied by other mood disorders). These are: schizoaffective disorder, delusional disorder, brief psychotic disorder.

Within schizophrenia proper, there used to be five subtypes. Because these provided no benefit in treating the disorder, they were retired and physicians now think in "dimensions": main negative symptoms, prominent hallucinations or delusions, disorganised speech or behaviour, catatonic features, and mood-related symptoms. It's good to name all of these!

It occurs typically between the late teens and early 30's. When it occurs before 17, it is early and after 40 it is late. After 60, it is termed very late. Usually, it is preceded by a prodromal stage (75%). Physiologically, there are competing dopamine and glutamate-based theories, which might well be combined. The idea of the first is that there are a number of misfiring dopaminergic neurons, which disturb the brain's salience. A decrease in D1-receptors in the dorsolateral PFC might be responsible for a decrease in WM. The glutamate theory bases itself on a disruption of cortical-thalamic communication. There is a noted overrepresentation of left-handed patients, and abnormal development of lateralisation. Heritability is high (60-80%). There is a large co-occurance of schizophrenia with anxiety disorders, OCD, substance use disorders, sleep disorders, DM2, autoimmune, and cardiovascular diseases (the latter 3 possible because of lifestyle and medication). People with schizophrenia are often excluded and victimised. Usually, they are only violence in relation to a comorbidity such as substance use; other than that, deficiencies in Theory of Mind may contribute.

Schizotypal personality disorder: (at least five of) ideas of reference, odd beliefs or magical thinking, unusual perceptual experiences, odd thinking and speech, suspiciousness or paranoid ideation, inappropriate or constricted affect, behaviour or appearance that is odd, lack of close friends or confidants, excessive social anxiety

Schizoaffective personality disorder: An uninterrupted duration of illness during which there is a major mood episode, unrelated 2 weeks of hallucinations and delusions (i.e., without concurrent mood disorder). There are the bipolar (mania and MDD episodes) and "just" depressive types.
