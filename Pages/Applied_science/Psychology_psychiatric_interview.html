Introduction

Traditioneel-gezien wordt er in Nederland onderscheid gemaakt in drie groepen mentale functies:

  1. Cognitieve functies: Bewustzijn, aandacht, oriëntatie, geheugen (korte- en langetermijn, en paramnesiën), oordeelsvermogen, realiteitsbesef, ziektebesef, en ziekte-inzicht, intelligentie/abstractie-vermogen, taal en rekenen, metacognitieve vermogens, voorstelling en waarneming, zelfwaarneming, en denken (vorm en inhoud)
  2. Affectieve functies: Stemming en affect, angst en vrees, emotieherkenning en -regulatie
  3. Conatieve functies: Praxis, executieve functies, psychomotoriek (mimiek en gestiek, spraak), motivatie, wil en gedrag

Daarnaast beoordeelt het psychiatrisch onderzoek:

  1. Eerste indruk: uiterlijk (lichamelijke kenmerken, leeftijdsschatting, kleding en zelfverzorging, gelaatsuitdrukking), (oog)contact en houding, klachtenpresentatie, en tegenoverdracht (gevoelens en reacties opgewekt bij de onderzoeker)
  2. Persoonlijkheid: algemeen, pathologisch, afweer- en copingsmechanismen, dysfunctionele schema's
  3. Mogelijk gevaar: suïcidaliteit, agressiviteit, en kindcheck
  4. Algemene aspecten: lijdensdruk, sociaal disfunctioneren

Specific situations

Agitation

De-escalation (Fishkind, 2011)

He establishes a 10-point domain:

  1. Respect personal space: at least two arms length out of personal safety, and because many patients have traumas regarding body or possessions
  2. Do not be provocative: avoid conceiled hands, do not directly face the patient, calm demeanor, and relaxed eye contact, do not appear uninterested. Congruent body language is important! At all cost, prevent coming across as humiliating!
  3. Establish verbal contact: only 1 person at a time, introduce yourself and provide orientation and reassurance
  4. Be concise: keep it simple, repetition is essential to successful de-escalation
  5. Identify wants and feelings: is it just ventilating, or medication, administrative intervention etc. You need to know, regardless of whether you can fulfill it!
  6. Listen closely to what the patient is saying: use active listening, use Miller's law ("to understand what another person is saying, you must assume that it is true and try to imagine what it could be true of i.e., how it could be true").
  7. Agree or agree to disagree: learn to agree "in principle" (instead of "yes the police has disrespected you" say "I believe everyone should be treated with respect".)
  8. Lay down the law and set clear limits: establish basic working conditions, limit setting must be reasonable and respectful, coach the patient on how to stay in control
  9. Offer choices and optimism: offer choices, and broach the subject of medication (e.g., what helps you normally)
  10. Debrief the patient and staff: debrief the patient, debrief the staff

Assessment (Stowell)

The initial assessment is focus on delirium: patient is confused and unable to focus, with perseverative behaviours, responding to visual halluciations, language/naming/cognitive impairments. If this is the case, and there is an underlying reason for delirium, then work with that.

The second, should be chronic cognitive impairment. So always do a MSE when possibble.

Third is psychosis, especially in the context of a known disorder. For this, collateral information is crucial. The same goes for mania.

Lastly, one may not forget drugs or drug withdrawal.

Psychosis

De-escalate as usual, prevent validating delusions but remain respectful and neutral in your questioning. There are a host of organic causes to rule out, such as: (follows BMJ best practice - Very good document in general)

  1. Primary psychiatric disorder: Schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform disorder, depression with psych. features, bipolar disorder, delusional disorder
  2. Secondary causes, such as:
    1. Iatrogenic: OTC (antihistamines, St. John's wort), prescription (Adrenergics, anticholinergics, corticosteroids, dopamine agonists, thyroid hormones), recreational drugs (alcohol, amphetamines, cannabis, cocaine, inhalants, phencyclidine)
    2. Exposure: heavy metal exposure, (organic) mercury, organophosphate exposure, asenic (ant killer, wood preservation)
    3. Neurological: epilepsy (8-times the risk), TBI, MS, brain tumours, dementia, stroke
    4. Infection: encephalitis (autoimmune or infectious secondary to herpes, HIV, measles/mumps/rabies, or bacteria/parasites/prion/fungi)
    5. Delirium
    6. Vitamin deficiencies: Thiamine (Korsakolff's psychosis), Niacin, Folate, Cobalamin (B1, 3, 9, 12)
    7. Endocrine: Cushing's, hyperparathyroidism, thymoma, thyroid dysfunction
    8. Autoimmune: a number are at a 45% increased risk of developing a chronic psychotic disorder
    9. Metabolic: Wilson's disease, lysosomal storage, homocystinuria, acute hepatic porphyria, metachromatic leukodystrophy
    10. Chromosomal: Klinefelter's syndrome, DiGeorge syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome

In sexually active population, think of HIV and syphilis!

Suicidality

A good assessment of suicidality starts from empathy, but is nonetheless direct. It assesses suicidal ideation, access to means, risk factors (past attempts, severe depression, substance use, and impulsivity), and protective factors (reasons for living, family support, religious beliefs). Here, the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) is useful:

  1. Have you wished you were dead or wished you could go to sleep and not wake up?
  2. Have you actually had any thoughts of killing yourself?
  3. Have you been thinking about how you might do this?
  4. Have you had these thoughts and some intention of acting on them?
  5. Have you started to work out or worked out the details of how to kill yourself? Did you intend to carry them out?
  6. Have you ever done anything, started to do anything, or prepared to do anything to end your life?

Anything beyond 3 is a moderate risk level, anything beyond 4 is a high risk level and needs admitting.
