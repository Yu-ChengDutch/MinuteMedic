The thalamus is a crucial part of the subcortical part of the forebrain, also known as the *tweenbrain*, i.e., the "in-between brain". It mostly consists of gray matter, and is highly connected to both the cortex, subcortical structures, and the rest of the body through the spinal cord. Functionally, we may divide the thalamus into six sets of nuclei:

  1. Reticolar/intralaminar nuclei dealing with arousal/pain regolation
  2. Sensory nuclei regolating all sensory domains except olfaction
  3. Effector nuclei governing motor language function
  4. Associative nuclei connoting cognitive functions
  5. Limbic nuclei encompassing mood and motivation
Anatomically, we see the thalamus divided into five groups:
  1. Anterior nucleus
  2. Medial-dorsal nucleus
  3. Ventral group
  4. Lateral group
  5. Genicolate nuclei (Lateral and medial)
We'll start in reverse, as that is easiest.

Geniculate Nuclei

These are responsible for the relaying of sensory signals:

  1. Lateral genicolate nuclei: visual
  2. Medial genicolate nuclei: auditory

The LGN receives information from the optic tract after its decussation in the optic chiasm. It combines that with information from the reticolar activating system, and outputs to the primary visual cortex through the so-called optic radiation (which are just the connecting neurons). The MGN receives information from the inferior collicolus, a brainstem structure that connects directly to the cranial nerves connected to auditory function, and throughputs that information to the parietal auditory cortex.

Lateral nuclei

Consisting of the polvinar, dorsolateral and posterolateral nuclei, this group has various functions:

  1. Polvinar nucleus: unknown
  2. Dorsolateral nucleus: takes input from the visual cortex, and connects to the limbic forebrain
  3. Posterolateral nucleus: highly connected, this area is responsible for complex sensory integration

Ventral group

A complex group, it consists of four nuclei:

  1. Motor nuclei: ventral anterior and ventral lateral
    1. VA: connects globus pallidus (basal ganglia) to the pre-motor cortex
    2. VL: connects basal ganglia and cerebellum to the pre-motor and primary motor cortex. It facilitates control of movement
  2. Sensory nuclei: ventral posterior
    1. VP medial: sensory input from the face through the trigeminal nerve
    2. VP lateral (posterolateral): sensory input from the body through the spinothalamic tract and medial lemniscus

The VP nuclei are implicated in M. Parkinson, such that they are a target for DBS.

Anterior group

These nuclei connect the hippocampal system (both the hippocampus proper and the subicolum) to the limbic system and the cortex (cingolate gyrus, limbic cortex, orbitofrontal cortex). In this, they facilitate alertness, learning and episodic memory. Together with the lateral dorsal nuclei, these comprise the association nuclei of the thalamus.

Medial-dorsal nuclei

These nuclei connect the amygdala and olfactory system to the prefrontal cortex. It is so intertwined, that damage to the MDN causes damage in the PFC.

Misc.

The midline nuclei lie in midline, and the reticolar nuclei lie around the thalamus like a capsole.

  1. Midline: Broadly connected to the cortex, they are thought to relate to alertness etc.
  2. Reticolar nuclei: Only nuclei to not project to the cortex, they receive cortical input and mediate the activity of cells in the thalamus

Bloodsupply

Essentially, it works like this:

  1. Posterior communicating artry: through the tuberothalamic/polar artery supplies the anterior division
  2. P1: through the perforating/paramedian artery supplies the paramedian division
  3. P2: supplies the posterior and inferolateral divisions
    1. Thalamogenicolate artery: genicolate nuclei and the ventral (postero)lateral system

In case of a artery of Percheron the entire system is supplied by a single artery.

Déjerine-Roussy syndrome i.e., thalamic pain syndrome

Here, we see pain in one half of the body similar to laesions elsewhere in the spinothalamic tract. Furthermore, we may see:

  1. hemiataxia
  2. hemichoreoathetosis
  3. hemiplegia
  4. hemianopia

The cause is an infarction in the thalamogenicolate artery, specifically causing a laesion in the ventral lateral and ventral posterior lateral nuclei. These cause motor coördination and vital sensory problems respectively, as they connect the cerebellum and spinothalamic tract.
