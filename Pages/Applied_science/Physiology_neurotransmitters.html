Introduction

We'll discuss the seven most important neurotransmitters:

  1. Adrenaline: Fight or flight
  2. Noradrenaline: Alertness and arousal
  3. Dopamine: Reward and pleasure
  4. Serotonin: Mood regulation
  5. Acetylcholine: Muscle activation and memory
  6. GABA: Inhibition and relaxation
  7. Glutamate: Excitation and learning

Monoamines

Monoamines are a class of neurotransmitters that contain a single amino group. They play crucial roles in various physiological and psychological processes, including mood regulation, arousal, and cognitive function. They include adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, and serotonin.

Adrenaline

Adrenaline is most associated with the "fight or flight" response, but is mostly synthesised in the adrenal glands. This adrenaline does barely cross the blood-brain barrier. Instead, it is responsible for the corporeal stress response. How does it then still influence the brain? Through stimulation of the N. Vagus, which in turn may stimulat the so-called Locus Coeruleus, which is an important nucleus for the noradrenergic response. Within the brain, adrenaline is synthesised by the C1-3 nuclei in the ventrolateral Medulla Oblongata. From there, it sends signals to the Thalamus and down through the Spinal Cord.

Noradrenaline

Noradrenaline is primarily synthesised in the Locus Coeruleus, which lies in the dorsal Pons. From there, it sends out long axons towards e.g., the PFC, amygdala, hippocampus, thalamus, and spinal cord. It is received by two types of receptor: alpha and beta. In general, alpha-1 and beta receptors enhance neurotransmission and plasticity, promiting stimulatory effects on the CNS, while alpha-2 receptors have inhibitory effects in the CNS such as reducing norepinephrine release and decreasing neuronal excitability.

Dopamine

There are three main dopaminergic pathways: the mesolimbic pathway, which is involved in reward and motivation, the mesocortical pathway, which is involved in executive function and working memory, and the nigrostriatal pathway, which is involved in motor control.

Mesolimbic Pathway

The mesolimbic pathway originates in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) of the midbrain and projects to the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, and hippocampus. It is associated with the experience of pleasure and reward, and dysregulation of this pathway has been implicated in addiction and other psychiatric disorders. Within the Dopaminergic-hypothesis, hyper-D2 activity in the limbic area causes the positive symptoms of schizophrenia.

Mesocortical Pathway

The mesocortical pathway also originates in the VTA but projects to the prefrontal cortex (PFC). It is involved in higher-order cognitive functions such as decision-making, attention, and working memory. Dysregulation of this pathway has been linked to cognitive deficits observed in conditions such as schizophrenia. Within the Dopamine-hypothesis, hypo-D1 activity in the PFC causes the negative symptoms of schizophrenia.

Nigrostriatal Pathway

The nigrostriatal pathway originates in the substantia nigra pars compacta and projects to the striatum. It is crucial for the regulation of voluntary movement, and degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in this pathway is a hallmark of Parkinson's disease.

Additionally, there exists the Tuberoinfundibular pathway, which allows dopamine to inhibit prolactin release. Blockage of D2 receptors, could thus increase prolactin, influencing lactation, libido, fertility, and bone health.

Serotonin

Serotonin originates in the Raphe nuclei of the brainstem. From here, there are two main pathways: the caudal raphe pathways and the rostral raphe pathways. The caudal nuclei supply serotonin to the brainstem and spinal cord, while the rostral nuclei project to the forebrain and modulate mood, sleep, and other functions.

Rostral Raphe Pathways

These include the dorsal and median raphe nuclei, and the projections blanket nearly the entire forebrain. It runs parallel with a GABAergic system, so that together they function as a subtle switch instead of merely on-off.

Acetylcholine

Acetylcholine is a neurotransmitter that plays a crucial role in both the central and peripheral nervous systems. In the CNS, it is involved in learning, memory, and attention, while in the PNS, it is essential for muscle activation and autonomic functions.

Cholinergic Pathways

The basal forebrain cholinergic system, which includes the nucleus basalis of Meynert, projects to the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, influencing cognitive processes. The brainstem cholinergic system, including the pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei, projects to various brain regions and is involved in arousal and sleep-wake regulation.

GABA

GABA (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid) is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. It functions by hyperpolarizing neurons, making them less likely to fire action potentials. There are three types of GABA receptor:

  1. GABA-A: Ionotropic receptor that mediates fast inhibitory synaptic transmission.
  2. GABA-B: Metabotropic receptor that mediates slow and prolonged inhibitory effects.
  3. GABA-C: Ionotropic receptor that is less common and primarily found in the retina.

These are spread wide throughout the brain.

Glutamate

Glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. It functions by depolarizing neurons, making them more likely to fire action potentials. There are several types of glutamate receptors, including ionotropic and metabotropic receptors. There are four major pathways:

  1. Corticostriatal pathway: from the cortex to the striatum, involved in motor control and habit formation.
  2. Thalamocortical pathway: involved in sensory processing and relay of information to the cortex.
  3. Corticothalamic pathway: involved in the regulation of thalamic activity and sensory information processing.
  4. Hippocampal perforant pathway: Connects the entorhinal cortex to the dentate gyrus within the hippocampus, forming the primary entry point for memory formation.
